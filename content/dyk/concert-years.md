# 1945 (the time the Blue Danube waltz was first performed)

**Kids: Did you know?** The concert’s most beautiful, most famous waltz, the Blue Danube waltz, was played at the New Year’s Concert for the very first time in 1945\. Ever since, it’s been one of the best-loved moments every single year.

**Adults: Did you know?** 1945 was the year the encore tradition began — and the year Johann Strauss II’s The Blue Danube waltz, today the concert’s signature waltz and a kind of unofficial Austrian anthem, was heard at the concert for the first time, as an encore, under the concert series’ founder Clemens Krauss. Remarkably, it entered the programme in the last winter of the Second World War. 

**Nerds: Did you know?** Introduced by Krauss, the Blue Danube has been an encore at nearly every concert since 1945 (with a few early exceptions), while the Radetzky March followed a year later, in 1946\. So the concert’s two iconic bookends were not present at the 1939 founding — they crystallised in the mid-1940s, exactly as the war ended and the event began the long process of shedding, and later confronting, its origins.

# 1959 (the time the concert was first televised)

**Kids: Did you know?** For its first twenty years you could only *hear* the New Year’s concert, on the radio. In 1959, for the very first time, people could *watch* it on television, and that’s when the whole world started tuning in.

**Adults: Did you know?** 1959 was the first time the New Year’s Concert was televised, which launched its internationalisation via the Eurovision network. Only the second half, following the interval, was broadcast at first, with conductor Willi Boskovsky was on the podium.

**Nerds: Did you know?** The televised second half of the series, a tradition which began with Eurovision in 1959, ran about 75 minutes. From 1966 the concert was also carried on Intervision, the Eastern-bloc counterpart to Eurovision, and regular overseas transmission was established starting in 1972, laying the technical foundation of today’s globally televised annual phenomenon. Television also reshaped the event itself. Today’s annual ballet interludes and the tightly directed camera work only exist because of it.

# 1968 (the time ‘Happy New Year’ went international) 

**Kids: Did you know?** One year — 1968 — the musicians didn’t just say ‘Happy New Year’ once, like usual during the encore. They said it in *nineteen different languages*, each time by a different member of the orchestra\!

**Adults: Did you know?** The spoken New Year’s greeting during The Blue Danube, a little moment when the conductor pauses to address the audience, was taken to a charming extreme in 1968\. Willi Boskovsky sent the New Year’s greetings to Europe and overseas in nineteen different languages, each voiced by a different member of the Vienna Philharmonic, with Boskovsky himself giving the closing English ‘Happy New Year.’ 

**Nerds: Did you know?** The greeting-during-the-Danube encore  is itself Boskovsky's invention, and 1968 (his fourteenth concert) shows how far he pushed the concert’s role as a gesture of international goodwill. Broadcast that year over both Eurovision and Intervision, ‘Happy New Year’ was wished in nineteen languages by nineteen individual orchestra members, reaching across the Iron Curtain at the height of the Cold War.

# 1982 (the time with a stage protest almost nobody noticed)

**Kids: Did you know?** Here’s a secret from 1982: some surprise visitors dashed onto the stage, completely naked, during the concert to hold up a sign on stage — but the millions of people watching at home on TV didn’t see a thing\! At that exact moment the cameras had switched to a pre-filmed ballet dance, so the orchestra just kept playing and viewers at home never knew.

**Adults: Did you know?** During the 1982 concert, conducted by Lorin Maazel, two activists, Florian Sommer and Robert Herz, stormed the stage of the Musikverein naked and unfurled a banner reading ‘Menschenrechte für Schwule’ \[‘Human rights for gays’\]. Almost no one watching at home saw it: the worldwide television audience missed the protest entirely because ORF happened to be airing a pre-recorded ballet interlude which had switched away at exactly that moment, as scheduled. 

**Nerds: Did you know?** The little-known 1982 *Neujarhskonzert* stage protest was actually a small landmark in Austrian LGBTQ history, not a simple prank. It was tied to the gay-rights movement (HOSI Wien) at a time when Austria still had highly discriminatory anti-gay laws. The two nude activists unfurled a ‘human rights for gays’ banner during Maazel’s concert, but the global TV audience saw a pre-recorded ballet sequence instead, meaning the disruption survives almost entirely in the movement’s own press (and the memories of orchestra and audience members sitting there live) rather than in the broadcast record. It’s a reminder that these ‘perfect’ televised concerts are highly choreographed constructions, and that it’s the cameras which really determine what history remembers.

# 2005 (the time there was no Radetzky March) 

**Kids: Did you know?** The concert almost always ends with a cheerful march that everyone claps along to. But in 2005 the orchestra chose *not* to play it,  as a way of showing respect and sympathy for the many people affected by a terrible tsunami.

**Adults: Did you know?** Days after the December 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami, the 2005 concert under Lorin Maazel closed with The Blue Danube and omitted the Radetzky March as a mark of respect to the victims. It is the only time the Radetzky March has been omitted in the series since it became a fixed way of closing the concert series. 

**Nerds: Did you know?** The 2005 omission of the Radetzsky March is the single known exception to a closing ritual that had been effectively inviolable since 1958, a rare case of current events from the outside world overriding the concert series’ near-liturgical form. It stands alongside 1967, when Boskovsky moved The Blue Danube into the main programme to mark the waltz’s centenary, as one of the only deliberate breaks in the sequence.

# 2021 (the time the concert played to an empty hall)

**Kids: Did you know?** In 2021 something had never happened before: the New Year's Concert was played to a completely *empty* hall\! Because people couldn’t gather during the COVID pandemic, around 7,000 fans from more than 90 countries clapped at home and sent the sound in over the internet, which was played through the speakers in the Golden Hall so the musicians could hear applause. 

**Adults: Did you know?** The 2021 concert, led by Riccardo Muti, was the first in the event's history to be performed without a live audience, because of the COVID-19 pandemic. Roughly 7,000 people from over 90 countries registered to send in their applause, which was piped into the empty Golden Hall through the sound system. The famous Radetzky March clap-along simply couldn’t happen, the first time that longstanding tradition was broken. 

**Nerds: Did you know?** 2021 was Muti’s sixth New Year’s Concert, and was played to an empty Musikverein under Austria’s COVID-19 pandemic restrictions. The virtual applause was transmitted into the hall only at the end of each half, not during the pieces, from a signed-up selection of the worldwide online audience, and the Radetzky March was performed as written, without the audience’s customary, rhythmic clapping.
